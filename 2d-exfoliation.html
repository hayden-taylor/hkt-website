Mechanics of exfoliating and transferring two-dimensional materials

Two-dimensional materials such as graphene, MoS2 and WS2 are held together by weak van der Waals forces, so single layers can be peeled from bulk crystals. Doing this reliably, with monolayers of controlled shape placed where they are needed, is a contact- and fracture-mechanics problem: the interfacial energies, strains and loading path decide which layer separates and where. Our work in this area began with molecular dynamics studies showing that superlubricity between compressed graphite and passivated silicon can thin graphite flakes, and with the printing of controlled folds into graphene using elastomeric stamps of patterned adhesion (with Trinity College Dublin).

With Daryl Chrzan’s, Joel Ager’s and Ali Javey’s groups at Berkeley we used atomistic calculations to explain how the strain induced by a thin deposited metal film influences whether one or several layers are exfoliated. We then developed a patterned-handle process that transfers lithographically defined monolayer MoS2 and WS2 features larger than 104 µm2 directly from multilayer sources. An exfoliate–align–release extension assembles arrays of predefined monolayers into van der Waals heterostructures. To characterize the results quickly, we showed that optical reflectance imaging, with co-optimized wavelength and oxide thickness, reveals interlayer coupling in stacked bilayers, and released the open 2dreflect software on nanoHUB.
